## The number that matters: this does not contradict T4c

T4c measured 1024×3 with 16 loops at **0.13/min** (58/60 clean) and that became
the shipping claim. This soak measured **0.93/min** on the same buffer and the
same loop count. That looked like a 7× regression on the longer window.

It is not. **T4c ran condition B; the soak ran condition D.** Both used
`SECONDS_PER_RUN=60`, so the windows are directly comparable — the conditions
are not. D adds the rest of the running stack on top of B.

The cost is consistent with the ladder measured at 512, where B→D was
2.27 → 3.13 (+0.86/min). Here B→D is 0.13 → 0.93 (**+0.80/min**). Same
direction, same magnitude. The stack costs roughly +0.8 xruns/min regardless of
buffer size, which is itself a useful finding: it looks like a fixed per-minute
cost, not something that scales with the period.

## What this changes

The "64 ms, up to 8 loops, clean" claim was measured **without the full stack
running**. Under condition D — what actually runs on the appliance — 1024×3
with 16 loops produces about one audible glitch per minute, sustained, for
eight hours.

Two consequences:

1. **The shipping claim needs re-qualifying.** Either state it as condition B,
   or re-measure the loop curve at 1024 under D. T4c's 0/4/8-loop cells were all
   0.00 at condition B; the 8-loop cell under D is unmeasured and is the one the
   claim rests on.
2. **The +0.8/min stack cost is now the largest single term** — larger than
   anything buffer geometry is likely to recover. T7a can still tell us whether
   the limit is cushion or deadline, but a win there does not remove this.

## Arrival statistics — the drain is random, not drift

Testing the 480 per-minute counts against a Poisson distribution (mean 0.927):

| xruns in a minute | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| observed | 199 | 164 | 80 | 31 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| Poisson expects | 190 | 176 | 82 | 25 | 6 | 1 | 0 |

Index of dispersion **1.091** (Poisson = 1.000). chi-square ≈ 4.0 over bins k=0..4.
About as clean a Poisson fit as real data produces.

**This rules out clock drift as the cause,** and with it an entire class of fix.

There are two distinct ways the ring buffer drains:

- **Excursion drain** — something occasionally delays the cycle. Arrivals are random and
  independent; each xrun triggers `snd_pcm_prepare`, which re-prefills the ring, so the
  budget resets on every glitch. This produces Poisson arrivals.
- **Drift drain** — the card's crystal and the system clock disagree. The Sound Blaster is
  a full-speed **adaptive** device with no feedback endpoint, so the host is guessing the
  sample rate; a steady mismatch drains the ring at a constant rate and underruns on a
  metronome. This produces **underdispersed** arrivals — regularly spaced, index well
  below 1.

The observed index is 1.09. Not drift.

**Consequence.** Adaptive-resampling bridges with a delay-locked loop (`zita-ajbridge`,
`alsa_out`/`alsa_in`) exist precisely to hold ring depth against drift. They would cost a
resampler on the hot path — against the CPU doctrine — to fix a problem this measurement
says is absent. Same for PipeWire's dynamic quantum, which additionally adds latency under
exactly the load where it would be noticed. Neither is worth spending on.

## The xrun is the refill

Worth recording because it explains the absence of clustering. The ring depth is set at
startup and never grows on its own — JACK writes exactly one period per wakeup, so the
gap between write and read pointer is a fixed offset, a budget spent rather than a
reservoir refilled. The mechanism that restores it is the xrun itself: JACK detects the
underrun, calls `snd_pcm_prepare`, and re-prefills to full depth.

Each glitch therefore pays for its own recovery, which is why 445 events appear as
isolated singles (199 clean minutes, worst minute 6) rather than a degrading spiral.
